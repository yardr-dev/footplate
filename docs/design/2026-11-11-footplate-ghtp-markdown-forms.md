# Tables, quotes, named links and rules in all three footplates

Design footplate-ghtp, proposed 2026-11-11. A proposal as it was decided on, not a description of what the code does today; the decision is on the bead.

Design for bead footplate-ghtp (flow `design.design`, stage `draft`). A yard is one installation of yardr: its beads, its crew and its depots. A bead is one durable piece of work with a stage and a history. footplate is the yard's native window on the desktop (Rust, gpui); footplate-ios and footplate-android are the same window on a phone. A letter is one message in a conversation. The light Markdown in this document is the small set of forms a letter is drawn with today: fenced and inline code, bullets and numbered lists one level deep, headings to the third level, bold and italic. A block is one piece a text is cut into and drawn as (a paragraph, a heading, an item, a fenced code block). yardr-design is the design language that stands beside the three apps: its tokens for colour, type, spacing and motion, copied into each app. See `docs/glossary.md` in the yardr repository for all of yardr's words.

## 1. The question

mckean picked "Both, places first" on ask 23575 (11 Nov 2026): first every description, card body and note draws what chat draws, then the new forms, each form on all three apps at once. The places are done (footplate-ios-0thy and footplate-android-ghna are merged; the desktop needed no places work). The question this document answers is the one the bead puts: which new Markdown forms — tables, quotes written with `>`, links written as `[text](url)`, and rules — are drawn alike in all three footplates, how is each one read, drawn and copied on each platform, and which forms stay as they were written, with what test wording shared across the three?

## 2. What exists today

**One light Markdown, ported rule for rule to three parsers.** The desktop reads a text in `blocks` in `src/markdown.rs`: a line scanner cuts the text into `Block`s of `Kind::Text`, `Kind::Code`, `Kind::Heading(1..=3)` and `Kind::Item { marker, depth }` (depth 0 or 1), and `inline` reads bold, italic and inline code inside a line, losing their marks in the drawn words and keeping them in the source. The parser is strict on purpose: a mark that opens or closes nothing stays as it was written, so globs (`*.rs`), paths (`src/my_file_name.rs`), `__init__.py` and sums (`2 * 3 * 4`) are never misread. Its test `what_only_looks_like_a_mark_is_left_alone` holds the forms this design is about as untouched text today: `"| a | b |\n|---|---|\n| 1 | 2 |"`, `"> quoted"`, `"---"` and `"![fence](fence.png) and <b>bold</b>[^1]"` are each one `Text` block, word for word. iOS ports it as `messageBlocks` in `Footplate/Model/Markdown.swift` (over Unicode scalars, where the Rust has bytes) and Android as `markdownBlocks` in `model/Markdown.kt` (over code points); both say in their first comments that a table, an image, HTML, a quote or a link's brackets stay the words they were. The three test suites already share their wording: the iOS `MarkdownTests.swift` and the Android `MarkdownTest.kt` port the desktop's cases one for one, names and sample texts alike, and the places beads added per-site cases in the same wording. That sharing is the mechanism this design's tests extend; there is no shared fixture file across the three repositories, and none is proposed.

**Each block knows where its drawn words stood, on the desktop alone.** A desktop `Block` carries its drawn `text`, the `source` range it stands for in the text as written, its `marks` in drawn coordinates, and a private list of pieces that maps every drawn boundary to a source boundary (`source_at`) and back (`shown`). That map is why the parser is its own and not a library (bead keaj's choice, recorded in the bead): a selection is kept as a stretch of the source, so what is copied has its marks. Phones keep no such map. An iOS `MessageBlock` and an Android `Block` carry kind, drawn text, marks in drawn coordinates and `apart` (a blank line stood before the block), and nothing about the source. Their copy rule is therefore split, on both phones alike: an explicit Copy of a whole text or note gives the text as it was written (`copyableProse` in `Footplate/UI/MessageText.swift`; the `CopyAction` in Android's `ui/Markdown.kt`, which puts `ClipData.newPlainText` of the source), a code block's own Copy gives the code between its fences alone (`copyText` on every platform), and a drag selection inside drawn words gives the drawn words, marks stripped, as it does today for bold and code alike. The desktop's in-text selection giving the source is a desktop property, not a shared one, and section 3.5 states the copy contract in those terms rather than promising one behaviour no phone has.

**Links today are bare addresses, found after the blocks are read.** No parser reads a link. On the desktop, `links` in `src/select.rs` finds `http://` and `https://` addresses in a block's drawn text, `shrink` replaces a link longer than 60 characters with its short form (`short_link` in src/markdown.rs: domain and the last 24 characters of what follows the host, scheme gone), `to_src` and `to_display` keep selection and highlighting honest past the shortening, `runs` draws a link in the palette's link blue with an underline, and a press and release on the same link opens it (`Clicked::Link`, `cx.open_url`). A shortened link's tooltip names the whole address. On iOS, `linked` in `Footplate/Model/Links.swift` cuts a drawn text into links and known-bead chips: only an explicit `http` or `https` address with a host, no user name or password, an ASCII host, and the sentence's punctuation after it left off (`web`, `withoutPunctuation`); `styleLinks` in `Footplate/UI/Controls.swift` draws a link underlined in the text accent and a chip in mono, in the text accent on the sunken surface; `proseLinks` hands a web address to the system and keeps a chip inside the app. On Android, `pieces` in `model/Links.kt` does the same cut (its `ADDRESS` regular expression, `hasHost`, `withoutPunctuation`) and `linked` in `ui/Linked.kt` draws it as a `LinkAnnotation.Url` with an underline, opened by `browse` in `MainActivity.kt` (a plain browser view, nothing comes back). The two phone checks are not the same check, and the difference matters once an address can hide behind words (section 3.4): iOS's `web` rejects an address with a user name or password and one whose host is not ASCII, while Android's `hasHost` asks only that something stand between `://` and the path, the query or the fragment, so `https://yardr.dev@evil.example/login`, whose host is `evil.example`, and a host written in non-ASCII characters both pass it today. A bare address shows itself, so that looseness costs nothing where it stands; a named link shows only its words, so it may not inherit it. On all three, a bare address is found in a block's drawn words wherever they stand, code included: the desktop runs `links` over every block's text in `text_inner` in `src/select.rs`, iOS's `linked` cuts its link ranges without consulting the `code` ranges it is handed (those ranges suppress bead chips, not links), and Android's fenced `CodeBlock` in `ui/Markdown.kt` calls `linked` for its contents under the comment that an address in code is a link. Three facts this design builds on: every platform already refuses any scheme but `http` and `https`; what a person sees in a bare link is the address itself, so there is no disguise to police; and a link's destination is known only in the renderer, because the drawn text still holds the whole address. A named link breaks that last fact — its destination is not in the drawn words — so it is the one form that must be read by the parsers and carried on the block, not found afterwards.

**What draws a block, per platform.** On the desktop, `text`, `verbatim`, `text_with_code_copy` and `text_with_refs` in src/select.rs cut a text with `markdown::blocks` and lay out one `StyledText` per block; `face` sets a heading bold and a little larger (1.25, 1.15, 1.05) and code in a mono face; `frame` puts 0.6 of a line above a block a blank line stood before or a heading, 0.3 beside code, an item's marker in its indent at the left; `paint` fills a code block's ground (`Palette.code`, a translucent lift), marks a wrapped line of code with `↪`, and draws a code block's Copy chip at its top right while the pointer is over it. The window's colours are one `Palette` in `src/theme.rs`: grounds, `line` for hairlines, `text` and `faint`, the yard rust `accent` with its readable `text_accent` step, `warn`, the link blue (`link`, `#7AA2F7` on dark and `#1A56C8` on light, held by its own test with the note that no design token names a blue), the peer violet, the running green `ok`, the rust-tinted `selection` and the translucent `code` ground. On iOS, `BlockView` in `Footplate/UI/MessageText.swift` draws a text or heading block as one `Text` of `styledWords` (roles from `ThemeType`: a first-level heading is the title, the rest the section; no italic face is bundled, so italic is slanted), an item as marker and words in an `HStack`, and a code block as `CodeBlock`: mono words on the sunken surface with a 6-point radius, scrolling sideways rather than wrapping, with a bordered Copy of 32 points in a 48-point target above it. Its gaps are the desktop's in points: 12 where a blank line stood or a heading stands, 6 beside code. `ThemeColor` in `Footplate/UI/Theme/ThemeColor.swift` is copied from yardr-design's `tokens/colors.css` by name. On Android, `Markdown` in `ui/Markdown.kt` draws the same shapes in Compose: `Words` for text and headings (`headingStyle` at the desktop's three scales, semibold), a `Row` for an item (marker at least 24 dp wide, 8 dp gap, 24 dp more for an item under another), `CodeBlock` on `surfaceDim` with a 6 dp radius scrolling sideways with its Copy above it, and `gapAbove` giving 12 dp and 6 dp by the desktop's rule. Its `Theme.kt` flattens yardr-design's tokens onto Material's roles by the same names. yardr-design's own rules that bear here: exactly one rust accent, green only for the running dot and red only for errors, counts as quiet words, hairlines between rows, no gradients or blur, phone targets of 48 dp and phone type roles at reading size. No new colour is needed by any form in this design, and none is proposed.

**The places are settled, so every form lands on one path per app.** The shaper's note on this bead (11 Nov, read at footplate@6f3bcba) verified the desktop: every place showing a description, card body or note already draws through `select::text` (`entry_body` and `bead_body` in `src/window/bead.rs`, `expanded_body` shared by the bead view and the asks, the ask context lines in `src/window/asks.rs`). footplate-ios-0thy swapped the five iOS sites from `linkedText` to `MessageText` and footplate-android-ghna swapped the Android sites from `Linked` to `Markdown()`; both are merged. A new block kind therefore appears in mail, descriptions, card bodies, notes and ask context at once, on each platform, with no second drawing path to keep in step — which is why mckean's order (places first) makes the forms cheap, and why this design specifies each form once per platform rather than once per place.

**What an agent's mail actually holds.** The forms were asked for because yard text holds them: the snowflake message held verbatim in all three suites (three numbered steps and one long fenced block) is the shape of an agent's instructions, and a bead's body or a run's output that reports results is where a table, a quoted instruction or a named link to a page stands. The window kit design (footplate-p4gg, decided alongside this one) records the run block's plan data as the same gap from the other side: the yard sends structured results and the card draws sentences, so a table in the words is the form a reader can already be sent today, with no yard change behind it. No socket call, protocol field or yard behaviour is needed by anything in this design; all three parsers read the text they are already handed.

## 3. The proposal

The smallest version that answers the question: four new forms read by the three parsers in the same strict manner as the forms that stand, drawn from the colours, grounds and gaps each app already has, and copied under the rule that already holds. Three block kinds are new — a table, a quote and a rule — and one inline form is new, the named link, which is carried on its block rather than found afterwards. Each form is specified as what makes it (its reading), what is drawn, and what a copy gives, per platform, with the sample set of section 3.6 as the one wording all three suites assert. What a person would type and see, for the four together:

```markdown
Two yards answer tonight:

| Yard | Waiting | Working |
| :--- | ------: | ------: |
| home | none | 3 |
| airy | 2 | none |

> Hold the autumn timetable until the folded
> carriers are printed.

See the [release page](https://yardr.dev/releases) before you start.

---
```

drawn as a ruled table with its head row set apart and its numbers at the right, a quoted passage set in from the left with its lines kept, the words "release page" as a link that opens the address, and a hairline where the rule stands — in a letter, a bead's description, a card's body and a note alike, on the desktop and on both phones. Nothing else a person types changes its drawing: a fence, a heading, an item, bold, italic and code stand as they stand, and any line the new readings do not claim stays text as it was written. Inside a fence, and inside an inline code span, the new forms are not read at all: a table's pipes, a quote's marker and a named link's brackets stand as the code's own characters, exactly as a heading's `#` does there today. One thing inside code is not new and does not change: a bare `http://` or `https://` address there is still found and opened as a link on all three platforms, as section 2 records, so the address inside a fenced `[release page](https://yardr.dev/releases)` still opens as a bare address does, while the brackets and words around it stay code and no named link is carried. Section 3.4 states the two apart, and section 3.6 samples both in one fence.

Two habits of the parsers that stand carry every form below, and are stated once here. First, strictness: a form is claimed only where its whole shape stands, and a shape that almost stands — a table with no delimiter row, a named link with no closing bracket, a run of quote markers that governs no words at all (section 3.2) — falls back to text as it was written, never to a half-drawn form with its marks stripped. Second, no nesting of blocks: as a heading's words and an item's words are read by `inline` alone today, a table cell's words and a quote's lines are read by `inline` (with the named link of section 3.4) and hold no block of their own. A fence, a heading or an item inside a quote or a cell stands as its written characters; section 4 names what that leaves for later.

### 3.1. Tables

**Reading.** A table is two or more lines read together, claimed only as a whole: a header line and its delimiter line are a whole table with no body rows, and each body line under them adds a row.

- A header line that holds at least one `|` and cuts into at least two cells. Cells are cut on a `|` that is neither escaped (`\|`, which stands in the cell as a `|`) nor inside an inline code span (a run of backticks opened and closed on the same line, as `inline` reads one), so `` `a|b` `` is one cell's code. A `|` at the line's very start or end frames the row and makes no cell. Each cell's words are trimmed of the spaces at their ends.
- A delimiter line directly under it, cutting into the same number of cells, each cell trimmed to an optional `:`, at least three `-`, and an optional `:` — `---`, `:---`, `---:` and `:---:`. Its colons say the column's alignment: none is left, a trailing colon alone is right, a leading colon alone is left, both is centre. Three hyphens is the floor because one or two read as a stray rule or a dash in prose, and the delimiter is the table's one unmistakable mark; a header with no delimiter under it, or a different number of cells, is no table, and both lines stay text.
- Body lines directly under the delimiter, each holding at least one `|`, until a blank line, a line with no `|`, or a line another reading claims first (a fence, a heading, an item, a quote or a rule). A body line is cut as the header is; a line with fewer cells is filled out with empty cells, and one with more has its extra cells left off, as a reader of the written rows expects. A table with a header and delimiter and no body lines is a table with no rows, drawn as its head alone.

A table is a new block kind. On the desktop it is `Kind::Table`, and the block carries its cells rather than one text: a header row and body rows, each cell an inline reading of its words — drawn text without marks, its marks in cell coordinates, and, on the desktop, its source range and pieces, cut from the line as `heading` and `item` cut their words today, so `source_at` and `shown` answer per cell exactly as they answer for a heading's words. On iOS it is `BlockKind.table` and on Android `BlockKind.Table`, each cell carrying drawn text and marks as a `MessageBlock` or `Block` does, with no source, as those blocks have none. A cell's marks and links stand in that cell's drawn words, never across a `|`; emphasis does not run from one cell into the next, as it does not run over a line break today. The table block's `apart` is the blank-line rule every block carries, and its spacing is section 3.5's. Its source on the desktop is the header's first character to the last body line's end (the delimiter line inside it), so a selection that begins in one cell and ends in another copies the written lines whole, pipes and delimiter included.

**Drawing.** All three draw the same table: a head row set semibold (the section face where a block has one), a hairline under it, body rows divided by hairlines, no box around the whole and no ground under the body, as rows elsewhere in the three apps are divided by hairlines and nothing else. A cell's words are set with 8 points (desktop: half a rem) of room at each side and 6 above and below, top-aligned so a wrapped cell does not pull its row's other cells down with it; inline code in a cell stands on the code ground in mono, bold and italic as marked, a link as section 3.4 draws one. Alignment from the delimiter moves the cell's words and its head together: left at the cell's start, centre in its middle, right at its end. On the desktop the table takes the reading column's width: each column is given the width of its widest cell's longest word-group up to a cap of two fifths of the column, the columns that remain share what is left, and a cell's words wrap inside its column as a paragraph wraps; a table that still cannot fit is not scrolled, because the desktop scrolls nothing in a letter today and wraps its code instead. On a phone the same grid is drawn at the phone's body size with the same cell room in points or dp; where the table's columns at that room would run past the screen, the whole table scrolls sideways as one piece, exactly as a phone's code block scrolls sideways today (`CodeBlock`'s horizontal scroll on both phones), with every column kept at no less than the width of its longest unbreakable word. It is never shrunk to fit and never restacked: what the person sees is the table that was written, reachable whole by one sideways drag, with its head row still its head. A table is drawn from its blocks alone, so it stands in a description or a note at the same width as in a letter, and a screenshot state that holds one (section 3.6) shows it in each place.

**Copy.** A table has no copy control of its own, on any platform: the code block's Copy is the one per-block control that stands, and a table is prose. The whole-text and whole-note Copies give the table as written, pipes, delimiter and all, as they give a list's markers today; the code block's rule (its Copy gives what stood between the fences) has no table twin. On the desktop a selection inside one cell copies that cell's written words with their marks, and a selection across cells or across the table's edge copies the source whole, as section 2 states; a selection is never laid out as tab-separated values, because the clipboard would then hold words nobody wrote. On the phones a drag selection gives the drawn words it gives today. TalkBack and VoiceOver read a table cell by cell in reading order; the head row's cells are announced as a header on iOS (`accessibilityAddTraits(.isHeader)`, as a heading block is) and carry the heading semantics on Android, and no platform invents a row or column count the words do not say.

### 3.2. Quotes

**Reading.** A quote is one or more lines in a row that each begin with `>` at the line's start, after no more than three leading spaces, claimed where at least one of those lines carries words. One `>` and at most one space after it are the marker and are not drawn; a line that is `>` alone, or `>` and spaces, carries no words, and inside a run that other lines give words to it is a blank line inside the quote and does not end it. A run in which no line carries words — a `>` standing alone, or several such lines together — governs nothing, so it is no quote: its lines stay text as they were written, under the strictness of section 3, as a heading marker with no words after it stays text today. A blank quoted line at a word-carrying run's edge stands as a blank line at that edge of the quote, as a blank line inside it does. The quote ends at the first line that does not begin so — a blank line without the marker, or any other line — and that line is read on its own; there is no lazy continuation, so a wrapped sentence a writer left unmarked stands outside the quote as its own text, which the writer can see and mark. One level is stripped: a line beginning `>>` gives a quote whose words begin with `>`, drawn as written, and a deeper quote is a later form (section 4). The lines that remain are read as one block's lines are today, breaks kept, each line read by `inline` and section 3.4: bold, italic, code and links stand inside a quote, and a heading's `#`, an item's marker or a fence's backticks inside one are their written characters. A quote is `Kind::Quote` on the desktop (an iOS `BlockKind.quote`, an Android `BlockKind.Quote`), its drawn text the stripped lines joined by line breaks, its marks and named links in drawn coordinates, its desktop source the first marker to the last line's end with the markers standing in the source and in no piece, as an item's indent already stands. A quote inside a list item, or an item inside a quote, is not read: whichever reading claims the line first — the item's, by the order `blocks` already tries readings — holds it whole.

**Drawing.** All three draw a quote set in from the text's left edge with a bar at its start: a bar 3 points wide (desktop: 3 pixels at the text's scale) in the strong hairline colour — the desktop palette's `line`, iOS's `borderStrong`, Android's `outline` — with the words one cell of room (12 points, 12 dp, three quarters of a rem on the desktop) to its right, in the text's own colour and face, at the text's size. Nothing else changes: the words are not made italic (italic stands only where it was marked), no ground stands under them, and the bar runs the quote's whole height, over a blank quoted line as over a written one. The bar is a hairline doing a bar's work, not the accent: yardr-design spends its one rust on what waits on the person and on primary controls, and a quotation is neither, so it takes the colour a divider already has on every platform and no new colour enters any palette. The quote's room above it is the common rule of section 3.5. On the desktop the bar and the inset stand inside the block's frame, so a selection's highlight runs under the words and not under the bar, as an item's marker stands outside its words today; the phones draw the same with a `Row` or `HStack` of bar and words, the bar drawn as a rounded-less rectangle of the hairline colour.

**Copy.** A quote has no copy control. The whole-text Copies give it as written, markers included. On the desktop a selection gives the one contiguous stretch of the source it covers, as every desktop selection does (`Selection::text` in `src/select.rs` is a slice of the text as written, and the quote's pieces map its drawn boundaries into that text with the markers standing in no piece), with no marker stripped out of its middle and none added at its ends. So a selection inside one quoted line copies that stretch of the line as written, its own line's marker not included; a selection that runs over a line break inside the quote copies the break and the next line's marker with it, exactly as it copies the marks between two selected words today — selecting the drawn words of `"> Hold the timetable.\n> It is printed."` whole, from its first drawn character to its last, puts `Hold the timetable.\n> It is printed.` on the clipboard, the first marker left off because the selection begins at the first drawn word and the second standing in the middle of the stretch — and a selection that begins in the block before the quote or ends in the block after it copies every marker between its boundaries, the first line's included. No new copy mapping is proposed to take the interior markers out: the contiguous source stretch is the mechanism that already carries bold, italic and code selections, and a clipboard text with its later lines' markers removed would be words nobody wrote, neither the drawn words nor the text as written. Section 3.5 states this once as the frame rule, and section 3.6's desktop samples assert the payload above word for word. On the phones a drag selection gives the drawn words, without `>`; the whole-text Copy is the as-written path, as it is for a list today.

### 3.3. Rules

**Reading.** A rule is a line that, after up to three leading spaces and any trailing spaces are left off, is three or more of one character — `-`, `*` or `_` — with a single space allowed between any two (`---`, `***`, `___`, `- - -`, `* * *`). It is tried before the item reading, and only where the whole line is the rule: `- - -` is a rule and not an item whose words are `- -`, while `- -` (two) and `- item` stay what they are today. A line of `=` is no rule and no heading, and a rule under a line of text makes no heading of it: the text stands as its own block and the rule as its own, so `Title\n---` draws a line of text and a hairline under it, not a second-level heading. That is a deliberate distance from the wider Markdown, taken because these parsers read line by line and a heading claimed by the line above it would make every underlined sentence a heading nobody marked with `#`. A rule is `Kind::Rule` (iOS `BlockKind.rule`, Android `BlockKind.Rule`): no drawn text, no marks, no links, its desktop source the line whole, its `apart` as any block's.

**Drawing.** All three draw a rule as a hairline across the reading column's whole width: 1 point in the subtle hairline colour — the desktop palette's `line` at its drawn weight, iOS's `borderSubtle`, Android's `outlineVariant` — with the common room of section 3.5 above and below it, and nothing else. It is the same line that already divides sections and stands under a table's head, at the weight those dividers have on each platform, so a rule reads as a break a writer put there and not as a control, a ground or a card's edge. No words stand on it, no control stands with it, and it is not selectable: on the desktop the selection passes over it (its source stands between its neighbours', and a selection from above it to below it copies the written `---` between the two, as a copy of two blocks copies the break between them), and on the phones it draws as a divider view with no text.

**Copy.** A rule copies nothing alone and is copied as written inside any wider copy, as a blank line is: present in the whole-text Copy, present in a desktop selection that spans it, absent from a phone's drag selection of drawn words, which has no words to take there.

### 3.4. Named links

**Reading.** A named link is read inside a line, by `inline` and its three ports, where a bare address is already given its room: the shape is `[`, the link's words, `](`, the address, and `)`, all on one line. The words are one or more characters up to the first unescaped `]` (a `\]` stands in them as `]`), read by `inline` again so bold, italic and code stand inside them and no link nests inside another; the address is one or more characters with no space and no control character in it, up to the `)` that balances the opening one, a `\(` or `\)` standing in it as the bracket. No title after the address is read: `[words](address "title")` holds a space, so it is no link, and the whole shape stays as written. The address must pass one check, the same on all three platforms, stated here once and implemented three times, and no platform's weaker standing check may stand in for it. What that check does is narrow: it guarantees that the address is a well-formed `http` or `https` address whose authority carries no user name or password, whose host is not empty and is written in ASCII characters, so the host a person would be sent to is the host that stands in the address and not one hidden behind an `@` or behind characters that read as a familiar name. What it does not do, and no check of the address alone can do, is establish that the link's words truthfully describe that host: `[release page](https://evil.example/login)` passes every clause above, and with the address hidden behind the words it still disguises its destination. The check is the whole protection against a malformed address and against a userinfo disguise, and it is no protection at all against a label that mismatches a well-formed host; whether that remaining risk is borne with the address one gesture away, or the host is shown beside the words so the mismatch stands in the line, is the operator's choice in question 3 of section 5, and section 3.6 names the `evil.example` address as a claimed link so that choice is made about a case in front of the reader. The address is read as a scheme, an authority and the rest (a path, a query beginning `?` and a fragment beginning `#`, any of which may be empty), and it is a named link only where all of these hold: the scheme is `http` or `https`, letter case aside, and is opened in lower case as the phones open a bare address today; the authority holds no `@` at all, so there is no user name and no password in it, empty or otherwise — `https://yardr.dev@evil.example/login` names the host `evil.example` and is refused here, however friendly its words; the host, the authority with an optional `:port` at its end left off, is not empty; and every character of the host is ASCII, so a host written in other characters, which can read as a familiar name while naming another host, is refused. What follows the authority is not trimmed or punctuated away: the address is the characters up to the balancing `)` exactly as written, sentence punctuation after that bracket being outside the shape already. iOS's standing `web` check in `Footplate/Model/Links.swift` is this check and may be reused as it stands. Android's standing `hasHost` is not: it asks only that the authority be non-empty, so the Android reading adds the `@`, host and ASCII conditions beside it (or in its place) and a named link is never accepted on `hasHost` alone, while a bare address there keeps the reading `pieces` gives it today. The desktop has no host check today — `links` in `src/select.rs` asks for a scheme and a character after it — so the desktop adds this check for the named form alone, and a bare address there keeps its standing reading too. Any other scheme (`mailto:`, `ftp:`, `javascript:`), a relative address (`/releases`, `#top`, `yardr.dev`), an empty address, an address whose authority holds `@` or whose host is empty or not ASCII, a missing bracket or a shape broken over a line is no link: the brackets, words and address all stay as written, as a mark that closes nothing stays today. An image's shape is the same shape with a `!` before it; the `!` is no part of any link here, so `![alt](address)` stays as written whole (section 4). Inside a fence, and inside an inline code span, the named shape is not read at all: a fenced block's words never pass through `inline`, and `inline` reads a code span whole before any link is looked for in it, so `` `[release page](https://yardr.dev/releases)` `` and the same line in a fence draw their brackets as code's characters and carry no named link. That is a statement about the new syntax only. A bare address inside the same fence or span keeps the behaviour section 2 records — found in the drawn words and opened on all three, in mono on its code ground — and the address characters inside a literal named shape in code are such a bare address to that standing search, so they still open; what code withholds is the collapsing of the words onto the address, not the address's own standing link.

A named link is carried on its block, because its address is not in the drawn words. Each block gains the links its reading found: on the desktop a list of drawn range, address and source range (the `[` to the `)`) on the `Block`; on iOS a list of drawn range in scalars and address on the `MessageBlock`; on Android a list of drawn range and address on the `Block`. A bare address keeps the path it has: it is still found in the drawn words by `links`, `linked` and `pieces`, and the two lists never overlap, because `inline` already steps over a bare address whole and a named link's words are read with the address left out. A bead id or an ask reference inside a named link's words is the link's words and no chip: the tap opens the address, and the reference readings that stand (`refs` on the desktop, the chip cut on the phones) skip what a named link covers, as they already skip what a bare link covers. Emphasis around a link (`**[words](address)**`) bolds its words and keeps it a link, as emphasis around a bare address does today.

**Drawing.** The link's words are drawn where the shape stood, with no brackets and no address, in the link dress each platform already has: on the desktop the link blue with its underline, shortened never (the words are the short form a writer chose, and `short_link` is not applied to them), the whole address standing in the tooltip while the pointer is over it, as a shortened bare link's tooltip names its address; on the phones underlined in the text accent, as `styleLinks` and `linked` draw a bare address, with the address carried as the link's accessibility value (VoiceOver and TalkBack announce the words, then that it is a link, then the host) and shown on a long press where the platform shows one for a bare link. Bold, italic and code inside the words stand with the link's colour and underline over them. A click or tap opens the address by the path that stands: `cx.open_url` on the desktop, the system's `openURL` on iOS with the chip scheme still intercepted before it, `browse`'s browser view on Android. Nothing is opened while drawing, while copying or while selecting, on any platform; a press that becomes a drag selects and opens nothing, as a bare link behaves today.

**Copy.** The words a copy gives for a named link are the shape as written, `[words](address)`, wherever the copy is an as-written copy. On the desktop the link's drawn words stand for the whole written shape in selection, as a shortened bare link's short form already stands for its whole address (`to_src` returns the shape's start inside it and its end at its end): selecting any part of the words selects the shape whole for copying, while the highlight draws over the words alone. On the phones, a whole-text Copy gives the shape as written with the rest; a drag selection gives the words as drawn, without brackets or address — the same drawn-words copy a bold word gives without its stars — and no phone grows a selection that reconstructs an address it cannot see. The address is never put on the clipboard alone by any copy of the words; a person who wants it opens it or copies the whole text.

### 3.5. What the four forms share: spacing, selection and the frame rule

**Spacing.** A table, a quote and a rule take the room the blocks that stand already take, by one rule on all three, which is `frame`'s rule on the desktop and `gapAbove`'s on the phones with the new kinds named where the heading is named. The wide room (0.6 of a line on the desktop, 12 points or dp on the phones) stands above a block where a blank line stood before it (`apart`), above any heading, above any table (its head reads as a heading does), and above and below any rule — a break with no room is a line in the text and not a break, so the block after a rule takes the wide room above it as if a blank line stood there. The narrow room (0.3 of a line, 6 points or dp) stands above a code block and above the block directly under one, where the wide room does not already stand. Anything else gives none. No form adds a gap inside itself except a table's cell room and a quote's quoted blank lines, which are its own lines.

**Selection and the frame rule.** A block's frame — an item's marker, a quote's markers and bar, a table's pipes and delimiter, a code fence — is never drawn as selectable words. The copies state it once for all forms, old and new: a whole-text or whole-note Copy on any platform gives the text byte for byte as written, frames included, and is the one copy that always does; a code block's own Copy gives what stood between its fences, frames excluded, and is the one per-block control; a desktop selection gives the one contiguous source stretch it covers, with a boundary at a frame standing outside the frame, so a selection of exactly one quoted line's words copies no `>`, a selection of exactly one cell copies no `|`, a selection over a quote's line break copies that break and the next line's `>` standing in the stretch's middle (section 3.2 gives the two-line payload word for word), and a selection that covers a whole block or crosses from one block into another copies the frames between the two boundaries, as it copies the line breaks between two lines of text today; a phone drag selection gives the drawn words it covers, frames and marks excluded, as it does today. No form adds a copy control, a share action or a context menu of its own; the menus that stand (a note's Copy note, a description's Copy description, a code block's Copy) keep their words and their payloads.

**Strictness is shared too.** Each platform's suite keeps its `untouched` habit for the new shapes: a shape that is not claimed is one text block, marks empty, drawn word for word. The fallbacks are part of the form's reading, not an error path beside it, and section 3.6's samples name them beside the claimed shapes, so a writer whose table has no delimiter sees prose, not a broken grid, on all three apps at once.

### 3.6. The shared sample set

One set of sample texts, used verbatim in all three suites, extending the ported cases that stand. Each suite adds its cases to the test that already drives the path — the desktop's tests in src/markdown.rs and src/select.rs, `MarkdownTests` with `MessageTextTests` on iOS, `MarkdownTest` with `MarkdownDrawnTest` on Android — as rows and steps of those tests, not as a parallel suite, and asserts the same things of the same strings: the blocks and their kinds in order, the drawn words of each block and cell, the marks and named links in drawn coordinates, `apart` where a blank line stands, and the copy each block gives. The desktop's suite alone also asserts the source ranges and `source_at`/`shown` round-trips, as it alone does today. The samples:

- **Tables.** `"| Yard | Waiting |\n| :--- | ------: |\n| home | none |\n| airy | 2 |"` (a head, right-aligned numbers, two rows); a table with bold, code and a named link in its cells; a table with a `` `a|b` `` cell and a `\|` cell; a ragged table (a short row filled, a long row cut); `"| a | b |\n| 1 | 2 |"` and `"| a | b |\n|---|---|\n| 1 |"` with their neighbouring claims (no delimiter, a body line with no `|` ending the table); a header and delimiter with no body (`"| a | b |\n|---|---|"`, a table of two lines with no rows, drawn as its head alone).
- **Quotes.** `"> Hold the timetable.\n> It is printed."` (two lines, one block; on the desktop, selecting its drawn words whole copies `Hold the timetable.\n> It is printed.`, as section 3.2 states, and selecting either line alone copies that line without its marker); a quote with bold, code and a named link; a quote with a `>` blank line inside it; `">"` and `"> \n>"` each untouched as written (a marker run governing no words is no quote); `"> Held.\nStanding outside."` (the second line its own text); `">> nested"` (one level stripped, the second `>` drawn); a quote directly under a heading and one after a blank line, for `apart`.
- **Rules.** `"---"`, `"***"`, `"___"`, `"- - -"` each a rule; `"--"`, `"- -"` and `"Title\n---"` not (text, an item's words or text, and text over a rule); a rule between two paragraphs, for its room.
- **Named links.** `"See the [release page](https://yardr.dev/releases) first."` (words drawn, address carried, no other mark); `"[yard](https://example.org:8443/a?b=c#d)"` (a port, a query and a fragment are a link, opened whole); a link with bold and code in its words; `"**[held](https://example.org)**"`; `"[mail](mailto:a@example.org)"`, `"[page](/releases)"`, `"[broken](https://example.org"`, `"[empty]()"` and `"![alt](https://example.org/x.png)"` each untouched as written; `"[yardr](https://yardr.dev@evil.example/login)"` (the authority's host is `evil.example`; the disguise stays written on all three, Android's `hasHost` notwithstanding), `"[yardr](https://user:pass@yardr.dev/login)"` and `"[yardr](https://münchen.example/login)"` (a user name with a password, and a host that is not ASCII) each untouched as written on all three; `"[release page](https://evil.example/login)"` (a well-formed address whose host the words do not describe: it passes the one check of section 3.4 and is claimed as a link on all three, its words drawn and its address carried and opened, so the sample states in the suite the very case question 3 of section 5 decides whether to leave hidden behind the words or to show the host beside); a named link beside a bare address in one line, each opening its own; a named link inside a quote and inside a table cell; and the two code cases that tell the new syntax from the standing bare-address reading: `"```\n[release page](https://yardr.dev/releases) and https://yardr.dev/releases\n```"` (one code block, no named link carried, its brackets drawn as code, and both address runs still opening as bare addresses do in code) and `` "`[release page](https://yardr.dev/releases)`" `` (one text block whose code span holds the shape as written characters, no named link carried, the address inside it still opening as a bare address does).
- **One whole text.** The example of section 3's opening, verbatim, asserted as its full block sequence (text, table, quote, text, rule) with every copy the platforms give, so the forms are proven together as a reader meets them and not only one by one.

Drawing is proven where each platform proves it today: the desktop by a screenshot state holding the whole-text sample in a letter and in a bead's body (`thread` and a bead state, in light and dark, through `src/shot.rs`), iOS by a drawn case in `MessageTextTests` and a screenshot in its line and decision tests, Android by `MarkdownDrawnTest` and its shot tests — each asserting that the table's head, the quote's bar and the rule's line stand where the blocks say, and that the sideways scroll of a wide table on a phone is one scroll of the whole table. No shot is taken from a deployed preview; each is taken from the app under test in its worktree, as the places beads took theirs.

## 4. Left out

**Images (`![alt](address)`).** The shape is a named link with a `!` before it, and it stays as written. Drawing it asks for fetching an address, sizing and placing a picture, an alt text's drawing, a failure's words and a save — the desktop already has a picture view for pictures a mail carries as data (in `src/window/bead.rs`), which is a different source from an address in the words. An image form is its own design when it is wanted; claiming the shape now as a link with a stray `!`, or as nothing, would hide writers' pictures in two different wrong ways, so the whole shape falls back to text.

**HTML, deeper headings, and the rest of the wider Markdown.** `<b>bold</b>`, headings past the third (`####`), setext headings (a line of `=` or `-` under text, refused in section 3.3), footnotes, reference links (`[words][id]` with a definition elsewhere), link titles, autolinks in angle brackets, strikethrough and task lists stay as written, for the reason the standing forms were chosen: the parsers hold the part an agent's mail uses, read strictly, and each addition must earn its reading against the texts that almost claim it. Nothing in the bead asks for them, and a table's delimiter or a quote's marker misread is a louder fault than a `####` drawn as words.

**Blocks inside quotes and cells.** A quote holds lines, not blocks; a cell holds inline words, not a list or a fence. Nesting would make each parser recursive — a quote's inner text cut into blocks again, with its own quotes and tables — for a shape agent mail rarely writes, and would reopen the source mapping (a desktop quote's inner block would need its own pieces inside the outer block's). One level of stripping and inline-only cells keep the three ports line-for-line alike, which is the property the shared samples police. A writer who needs a list quoted writes it outside the quote today and still can.

**A table as data.** No sorting by a column, no copying a table as tab-separated values or as a spreadsheet, no editing, no headerless table, no column the writer did not write. A table is drawn prose, as a list is; the yard's structured results (the run plan of footplate-p4gg's note) are the place for data a person acts on, and they arrive as data, not as pipes in a sentence.

**A new colour, ground or control.** No form takes the rust accent, a sunken card ground or a filled surface, and no form adds a button, a menu or a setting. The quote's bar and the rule are hairlines that stand; the table is rows and hairlines that stand; the named link is the link dress that stands. yardr-design gains no token from this design, and no app's palette changes: the desktop's link blue remains the colour its test holds, with no token naming it, as it is today.

**Changing what stands.** Bare addresses keep their readings, their shortening and their openings exactly; `verbatim` texts (an asker's marked words) keep holding no block and no link; code keeps its Copy and its sideways scroll on the phones and its wrap on the desktop. A form's fallback is always the drawing that stands today, so a text the new readings misjudge reads as it read before this design, never worse.

## 5. Open questions

1. **A wide table on a phone: scroll, shrink or restack?** Option A, as proposed in section 3.1: the whole table scrolls sideways as one piece, as a phone's code block does — costs a reader one sideways drag to see the far columns, and a very wide table is read in two positions. Option B: shrink the words and cell room until it fits — costs legibility at exactly the width a table is widest, and a size no other words on the screen take. Option C: restack each row as its head's labels over its cells, one row under another — costs a second drawing of a table that exists nowhere else, a head repeated on every row, and copies and selections that no longer meet the written rows. I would take A: it is the answer both phones already give to a wide block of code, it keeps one table drawing on all three apps, and what is written stays what is seen.
2. **A quote's words: the text colour behind a hairline bar, or quieter?** Option A, as proposed in section 3.2: the words keep the text's colour and face, the bar is the strong hairline — costs a quote that reads at the same weight as the words around it, told apart by its bar and inset alone. Option B: the words in the secondary colour (`faint`, `textSecondary`, `onSurfaceVariant`) behind the same bar — costs quoted words that read as metadata, which a long quotation (an instruction quoted whole) should not, and a second meaning for the secondary colour beside the metadata it already carries. Option C: the words on the sunken ground with the bar as its edge — costs a ground yardr-design spends on code and chips, and a quote that reads as a card among prose. I would take A: a quotation is the letter's own words set apart, not a lesser kind of words, and the bar and inset already say so.
3. **A named link's address: hidden behind its words, or shown beside them?** No option makes the words trustworthy: the check in section 3.4 proves the address well-formed with its host standing plainly in it, and proves nothing about whether the words describe that host, so `[release page](https://evil.example/login)` is a link under every option and the choice is only whether its mismatch stands where a reader can see it. Option A, as proposed in section 3.4: the words alone are drawn, the address in the tooltip on the desktop and in the link's accessibility value on the phones — costs a reader who wants the address one hover or long press, accepts that a well-formed address behind mismatched words (`release page` opening `evil.example`) reads as its words until that gesture is made, and gives a printed or shot letter that does not show where its links go at all. Option B: the words with the host after them in the secondary colour (`release page · yardr.dev`) — costs a line that breaks where the bare address beside it does not, and a host that settles the host but not the path (`yardr.dev` over an address that goes elsewhere on that host); its gain over A is that the `evil.example` mismatch stands in the line itself, with no gesture. Option C desktop-only: shorten the address under the words as a second line — costs the phones a form they cannot copy at their width and the three apps a link that draws two ways, for the same sight of the host that B gives on all three. I would take A, accepting the mismatch risk as stated: the writer chose the words as what is read, the address one gesture away is the standing answer for a shortened bare link, and a reader who follows a named link from a letter they already trust is in the same position as one who follows a bare address shortened past its path. Taking B instead is the honest alternative if that risk is not accepted; what no option may rest on is the claim that the reading checks police the disguise, because they police the address's shape and not its description.
4. **A table's columns on the desktop: measured from its cells, or equal shares?** Option A, as proposed in section 3.1: each column takes its widest cell's measure up to two fifths of the reading column, the rest share what remains — costs a column width that moves as the widest cell changes, so two tables in one letter may rule their columns differently. Option B: equal shares of the column for every table — costs a one-word column given a third of the page and a sentence's column wrapped into a ribbon, the two faults a table is written to avoid. Option C: no wrap at all, the table scrolling sideways on the desktop as on a phone — costs the desktop its one rule that a letter's blocks wrap and never scroll, which its code wrap and its `↪` mark already settled. I would take A: a table's columns are its writer's, measured from what stands in them, and the cap keeps one loud cell from taking the page.
